Scope: usability evidence applicable to a dense, keyboard-heavy, Turkish-language Qt desktop tool used in long sessions by older, non-technical staff.

## TL;DR

- Response time: 0.1 s feels instant, 1 s keeps the flow of thought, 10 s is the attention limit (Miller 1968; NN/g) [1] (secondary). For longer waits NN/g recommends a percent-done indicator, which is the most informative kind of wait feedback [1a] (secondary). The "show a bar with time remaining and cancel" part is our own design rule, not NN/g wording.
- WCAG 2.2 AA pointer targets: at least 24x24 CSS px (or spacing so a 24 px circle does not overlap neighbours) [5]. For older users aim much higher (about 32-40 px on desktop for primary controls; AAA 2.5.5 says 44 px) [2][6].
- Fitts's law: time grows with distance/size ratio; large, near, edge-anchored targets win. Keep frequent actions big and close to where the eye/hand already is [7].
- Hick's law: choice time grows logarithmically with the number of options; show few primary actions, push rare ones into menus [7].
- Automation UX: staff reviewing machine output suffer complacency and automation bias; the literature shows omission and commission errors, and that training alone does not remove them [8][8a] (abstract-level, secondary). Mark uncertain OCR values visibly, make review exception-based but with sampling of "confident" items, and never auto-accept silently. No study of human error detection on OCR output specifically was found (gap).
- Prefer undo over confirmation dialogs; use confirmation only for irreversible actions, with specific verbs on the buttons. This is widely argued (Cooper; Smashing; UXmatters) [3a] (secondary); a primary NN/g page stating it could not be fetched, so do not attribute it to NN/g (unverified).
- Keyboard-first: every action reachable by keyboard, visible shortcut hints, logical focus order, no hidden modes, never rely on Turkish-specific characters in shortcuts [10][11].
- Accessibility floor (verified against WCAG 2.2 text [6]): text contrast 4.5:1 (1.4.3 AA; 3:1 for large text), 7:1 enhanced (1.4.6 AAA), non-text/UI contrast 3:1 (1.4.11 AA), focus never entirely hidden (2.4.11 AA), focus indicator at least a 2 CSS px thick perimeter and 3:1 change between focused and unfocused pixels (2.4.13 is AAA, adopt voluntarily) [4][6].
- Older adults: bigger text, bigger targets, explicit labels, fewer hidden interactions. NN/g's 2013 web study (secondary): seniors 55.3% task success vs 74.5% for ages 21-55, 7:49 vs 5:28 per task (about 43% longer), 2.4 vs 1.1 errors [2]. These are website numbers, not desktop-app numbers.
- Turkish: casing must be locale-aware (I/ı, İ/i); general UI text expansion guidance is about 30-35% on average but 100-200% for strings of 1-10 characters (IBM table as reproduced by secondary sources; no Turkish-specific figure found) [22]; Q is the layout used on most Turkish computers, F share unverified, so shortcut letters need care.

## 1. Nielsen's 10 heuristics applied to Evrakçı [12]

1. **Visibility of system status.** Every tab shows job state: scan imported (n/total pages), OCR running (page k of n), split pending review, export complete. A persistent status strip shows the current job, unsaved/saved state and background tasks.
2. **Match with the real world.** Use office vocabulary (evrak, tutanak, dosya, sayfa), not technical terms (OCR, regex, bounding box). The "Değerler" table should mirror the paper form's field names.
3. **User control and freedom.** Undo/redo in Kontrol exists; extend to Değerler edits and template changes. Always provide a way out of a modal state (Esc).
4. **Consistency and standards.** Same shortcut and same verb for the same action across tabs; follow Windows conventions (Ctrl+Z, F2 to edit cell, Enter to commit, Esc to cancel, F6 for pane switching).
5. **Error prevention.** Block export while unresolved items remain, or state the count; prevent duplicate names; disable impossible actions rather than erroring.
6. **Recognition rather than recall.** Show the page image next to the value being verified; show shortcut hints in tooltips and button labels; the Rehber is available contextually, not just as a tab.
7. **Flexibility and efficiency.** Mouse path for novices plus keyboard accelerators for experts; "next uncertain item" command.
8. **Aesthetic and minimalist design.** Dense does not mean cluttered: only show data relevant to the decision at hand (see section 10).
9. **Help users recognize, diagnose, recover from errors.** Plain Turkish messages: what happened, why, what to do; no codes as the primary message.
10. **Help and documentation.** Task-oriented Rehber, searchable, with images; contextual "?" per tab.

## 2. Response-time limits and progress indicators

NN/g (after Miller 1968 and Card et al. 1991) gives three limits [1] (secondary: NN/g topic page and search summaries; the three values 0.1/1/10 s and their origin in Miller 1968 are confirmed, the Card et al. 1991 co-citation is from memory, unverified):
- **0.1 s**: feels instantaneous, supports a sense of direct manipulation. Target for selection highlight, focus move, cell edit echo, thumbnail selection.
- **1.0 s**: users keep a feeling of control and flow of thought; they notice the delay. Between 1 and 10 s users feel "at the mercy of the computer" (secondary). Whether an indicator is needed below 1 s is not settled by NN/g in what could be read; a secondary source (Zurb) suggests a spinner for 100 ms-1 s (unverified).
- **10 s**: attention ceiling; past it the experience is poor and users switch tasks. NN/g recommends a percent-done indicator for longer waits (secondary).

NN/g's progress-indicator article distinguishes two types, looped animation and percent-done, and says percent-done is the most informative [1a] (secondary). The specific thresholds below (spinner for 1-10 s, determinate bar over 10 s) are NOT stated as a formal NN/g rule (the search summary explicitly found none); treat them as our design policy: under ~1 s no indicator; 1-10 s a looping spinner or busy indicator; over 10 s a determinate progress bar (percent or "k of n"). For batch work of 100-800 pages (OCR, export), show "page 143 / 612", elapsed and estimated remaining time, allow cancel and allow the user to keep reviewing finished pages while the rest processes (skeleton content, not blocking).

Qt note: never do OCR or PDF work in the GUI thread; move work to QThreadPool/QThread and emit progress signals. A blocked GUI thread beyond ~100 ms is perceived as lag (consistent with the 0.1 s limit); Windows declares a window hung when the UI thread has not processed messages for 5 seconds, and Microsoft states users get annoyed after a couple of seconds and try to terminate a hung application after 5 seconds [23] (verified).

## 3. Fitts's law and Hick's law

- **Fitts's law** (Fitts 1954; MacKenzie 1992 Shannon form): MT = a + b * log2(D/W + 1) [7]. Larger and closer targets are faster. Screen edges and corners behave as effectively infinite-size targets on a desktop, hence menu bars/toolbars at the window edge are fast, but floating toolbars in the middle are not.
- Implications: primary actions (Accept, Next, Split here) should be large (not tiny icons), placed near the content the user is looking at, and adjacent to each other only when they are not mutually destructive. Separate destructive buttons by space (not just color). Do not shrink buttons to fit more on a 1366x768 screen; instead reduce secondary chrome.
- **Hick's law** (Hick 1952, Hyman 1953): decision time T = b * log2(n + 1) [7]. Fewer visible choices at one level speeds choice, but only helps when choices are unordered; with ordered, familiar items (menus users know) the effect is smaller. Practical rule: at most about 5-7 primary actions per toolbar group; group and label the rest.
- Do not misuse "7 plus or minus 2" as a hard limit on menu items; it concerns short-term memory, and Cowan (2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1):87-114) argues Miller's number was a rough rhetorical estimate and the real limit is about 4 chunks [13] (abstract verified via search).
- The Fitts and Hick formulas and the "5-7 primary actions per group" figure are textbook/own heuristics, not re-verified in this pass (unverified).

## 4. Cognitive load and working memory in repetitive verification

- Working memory holds about 4 chunks (Cowan 2001) [13]. A reviewer comparing an OCR value to the page image must hold the field name, the printed value and the proposed value. Keep these co-located (same screen area, ideally the page crop displayed directly above or beside the editable cell) so nothing is held in memory (split-attention effect, Sweller's cognitive load theory) [14].
- Repetitive tasks cause vigilance decrement. Secondary summaries (Wikipedia; a National Academies chapter) report that detection accuracy in Mackworth's clock test falls notably after about 30 minutes, that a decrement can be significant within the first 15 minutes, and under demanding conditions within the first minutes [24]. The exact curve depends on task, and signal-detection analyses attribute much of the decrement to a change in response criterion rather than sensitivity in most, not all, cases (secondary). Not measured for OCR review; transfer to this domain is (unverified). Mitigate with pacing: progress counter ("183 / 612 kontrol edildi"), natural breakpoints between documents, and reminders for breaks (optional, non-modal).
- Reduce extraneous load: the same layout on every item, stable positions of controls (no layout shift after load), consistent focus landing point after each action (e.g., focus returns to the next unresolved row).
- Use progress and "chunks" (per document, per batch) to give a sense of completion (goal gradient effect).

## 5. Human-in-the-loop and automation UX

Key findings from the automation literature:
- **Automation complacency and bias**: Parasuraman and Manzey (2010, Human Factors 52(3):381-410) conclude that complacency and automation bias are overlapping manifestations of automation-induced attention effects; complacency occurs when manual tasks compete with the automated one for attention, appears in novices and experts, and is not removed by brief practice; bias produces omission and commission errors with imperfect aids, and training/instructions do not prevent it [8] (abstract-level, secondary). Skitka, Mosier and Burdick (1999, Int. J. Human-Computer Studies, 991-1006) found both error types with an automated aid, and NASA follow-ups found accountability reduced them [8a] (secondary). That complacency is greatest when the automation is mostly reliable is a common statement in this literature but was not confirmed here (unverified).
- **Trust calibration**: show the system's confidence and reasons so users can calibrate (Lee and See 2004, Human Factors 46(1):50-80). Their model links calibration, resolution and automation capability, with trust grounded in performance, process and purpose; misuse and disuse are the failures of inappropriate reliance [9] (abstract verified via search). Trust should match actual reliability, not be maximised.
- **Evidence gap on OCR specifically**: a targeted search found no study that measures how many OCR errors human reviewers miss or automation bias in OCR review. Adjacent work: confidence-based human post-correction of handwritten-text recognition reported near-human accuracy (2.14% CER) at 66% lower cost and 83% higher throughput (2025 arXiv paper, snippet only, not read; unverified). Treat the audit-sample rate below as something to measure in our own pilot, not a literature value.
- **Showing uncertainty**: low-confidence OCR values should be visually distinct (not only by color: add an icon or label and a sort/filter), with a numeric or three-level confidence (e.g., Yüksek / Orta / Düşük). Do not display raw model scores users cannot interpret; tune thresholds on real data (unverified for Evrakçı; to be measured).
- **Exception-based review**: reviewing only low-confidence items is faster but invites misses on confident-but-wrong values. Mitigations from document-capture practice (e.g., commercial IDP products; unverified specifics): sample a fraction of high-confidence items for audit, run cross-field validation (date formats, ID checksum, duplicates), and require explicit first-pass review of the first N documents of each new template.
- **Alert fatigue**: if too many items are flagged, users stop reading flags. In clinical decision support, drug-drug-interaction alert override rates are very high: a 2022 scoping review reports 55-98% and a 2025 meta-analysis preprint a pooled 79.8% (95% CI 73.0-86.7%, individual studies 55.4-95.7%) [25] (secondary; the earlier "49-96%" figure sometimes quoted for van der Sijs 2006 could not be confirmed and the 49-73% range in that paper's table belongs to allergy alerts). Different domain, so only an analogy. Keep flagged proportion low via calibrated thresholds, and tier severity.
- **No silent acceptance**: "accept all suggestions" must show a count and be undoable; the export step should list unreviewed items and refuse or warn clearly.
- Make correction cheap: accept suggestion = one key; edit = start typing; revert to OCR value = one key.

## 6. Keyboard-first design for expert repetitive work

- Provide a complete keyboard path; visible shortcut hints in menus/tooltips (discoverability); a command palette or searchable shortcut list (Ctrl+K style, optional) [10].
- Predictable focus order (left to right, top to bottom; matches visual order; WCAG 2.4.3 Focus Order and 2.4.7 Focus Visible) [6]. Avoid focus traps; Esc always cancels.
- **Mode errors** (Norman; Raskin "The Humane Interface"): a key meaning different things in different states causes errors. Where modes exist (e.g., "draw box" in Şablonlar), show a persistent, visible mode indicator and use a distinct cursor; escape returns to the neutral mode.
- Single-key shortcuts conflict with typing in editable cells; require them only when focus is not in a text field, or use modifiers (WCAG 2.1.4 Character Key Shortcuts, Level A, verified: if a shortcut uses only letter, punctuation, number or symbol characters, at least one of: a mechanism to turn it off, a mechanism to remap it to include a non-printable key such as Ctrl or Alt, or active only when the component has focus) [6]. Note WCAG is written for web content; applying it to a Qt desktop app is by analogy (WCAG2ICT), which this pass did not check.
- Typing in a cell should replace/edit predictably (F2 to edit existing, Enter to commit and move down, Tab to move right), as in spreadsheets users know.
- Allow reading speed: auto-advance after accept (move to next row) as a user preference.

## 7. Error prevention, undo vs confirmation

The case against confirmation dialogs is well supported in secondary sources: users habituate and click through ("dialog fatigue"), undo does not interrupt flow, and dialogs work only when rare and for truly irreversible actions (Cooper as quoted in Smashing "Confirm or Undo"; UXmatters, Hoober 2020 "Are You Sure? vs. Undo") [3a] (secondary). The earlier attribution of this to an NN/g "User Control and Freedom" article could not be verified (NN/g unreachable); NN/g heuristic 3 (user control and freedom, support undo/redo) is the closest primary basis [3] (unverified wording). Use a confirmation only for actions that are truly irreversible or costly (delete job from disk, overwrite exported files). When used, label buttons with the specific verb ("Dosyaları sil", "Vazgeç") rather than Yes/No/OK; make the safe option default; state consequence and count ("12 belge silinecek"). Consider a transient "Geri al" toast for 5-10 s (duration is a convention, unverified) for reversible actions. Multi-level undo with a visible history is valuable in Kontrol.

## 8. Older adults and low vision

- NN/g's senior-citizen studies: the 2002 study ("Why Design for Older Adults?") and the 2013 follow-up ("Usability for Senior Citizens: Improved, But Still Lacking"; the article at the older URL is now titled "Usability for Older Adults: Challenges and Changes") [2] (secondary, via search summaries). Reported numbers: 2013 study with 31 participants over 65 on 29 websites and a control group of 20 users aged 21-55; success rate 55.3% (seniors) vs 74.5% (younger), about 26% lower; time on task 7:49 (2013 seniors) vs 5:28 (younger), about 43% longer, and 9:58 in 2002; errors 2.4 vs 1.1; when in trouble seniors blamed themselves 90% of the time vs 58% of younger users; seniors gave up more slowly; readability and text size remained a recurring problem across studies; drastic design changes hurt seniors most; NN/g estimated up to 35% more e-commerce sales from better senior UX. Participant counts and the 35% figure come from one search summary and should be re-checked on the page. These are web results and need not transfer to desktop apps.
- Text: avoid text below ~14 px at 96 dpi (about 10.5 pt) for body text; for this audience default to 14-16 px (about 11-12 pt) and allow scaling (our own guideline; no primary source found for a numeric minimum, unverified; Windows default UI is 9 pt Segoe UI, i.e., 12 px, which is small for weak eyesight; Segoe UI 9 pt not re-verified). Respect OS DPI scaling and provide an in-app text size setting (e.g., 100/125/150%).
- Contrast: WCAG 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), Level AA, requires 4.5:1 for text and images of text, 3:1 for large-scale text; "large scale" is at least 18 point or 14 point bold (WCAG definition) [6] (verified). Exceptions: inactive UI components, pure decoration, logotypes. WCAG 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced), Level AAA, requires 7:1, and 4.5:1 for large text [6] (verified). For older users aim for 7:1 for body text. Aging eyes need roughly 2-3 times more light/contrast than youth (general vision literature; unverified quantification).
- 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (Level AA, verified): visual information required to identify user interface components and states (except inactive components or where the appearance is set by the user agent), and parts of graphics required to understand content, need at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s) [6]. This applies to input borders, checkboxes, icon-only buttons, and selection outlines on thumbnails.
- 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), Level AA, new in 2.2 (verified): target size for pointer inputs is at least 24 by 24 CSS px, except Spacing (undersized targets positioned so a 24 px diameter circle centred on each one's bounding box does not intersect another target or another undersized target's circle), Equivalent (same function via another compliant control), Inline (target in a sentence or constrained by line-height), User Agent Control, and Essential [5][6]. The Understanding page says the Working Group chose 24 px as a compromise after reviewing average target sizes, while 2.5.5 Target Size (Enhanced), Level AAA, uses 44 by 44 (with exceptions Equivalent, Inline, User Agent Control, Essential) [5][6]. Recommendation: toolbar buttons at least 32x32 px with 8 px spacing; primary buttons at least 40 px tall.
- 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum), Level AA (verified): when a UI component receives keyboard focus, the component is not entirely hidden due to author-created content (2.4.12, AAA, strengthens this to "no part hidden"). Content the user opened may obscure it if the user can reveal it without moving focus [6]. Relevant to a table with sticky header and a docked panel: scroll the focused row clear of them.
- 2.4.13 Focus Appearance, Level AAA in WCAG 2.2 (verified): when the focus indicator is visible, an area of it is at least as large as the area of a 2 CSS px thick perimeter of the unfocused component or sub-component (grid cells and menu items count as sub-components) and has at least 3:1 contrast between the same pixels in focused and unfocused states; exceptions for user-agent-determined indicators [4][6]. The Understanding page notes many people, including older people, cannot see small changes in appearance. Not required but cheap to adopt; adopt it. (2.4.7 Focus Visible is Level AA, 2.4.3 Focus Order Level A.)
- 1.4.1 Use of Color (Level A, verified): color is not the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response or distinguishing a visual element [6]. About 8% of men have color vision deficiency (commonly cited figure; unverified for Turkish population).
- 1.4.4 Resize Text (Level AA, verified): text can be resized up to 200 percent without assistive technology without loss of content or functionality [6]; test the layout at 150-200% on a 1366x768 screen.
- Avoid time-limited toasts for essential information. (WCAG 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable, Level A, concerns time limits set by content and requires turn-off, adjust over at least 10x, or an extend warning of at least 20 s with up to ten extensions; applying it to toasts is our interpretation.)

## 9. Eye strain, long sessions, light vs dark

- Positive polarity (dark text on a light background) gives better performance than negative polarity. Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund and Buchner (2013, Ergonomics 56(7):1116-1124, "Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults") tested visual acuity and a proofreading task and found a positive-polarity advantage in both age groups; the authors had expected age-related light scatter to reverse it in older adults, but it did not, and they recommend positive polarity independent of age [15] (abstract-level, secondary). The pupil-size / depth-of-field explanation in the earlier draft is not what the later work supports: Buchner et al. and Piepenbrock et al. (2014, "The advantage of positive text-background polarity is due to high display luminance", Ergonomics; title verified, details not) attribute it to display luminance [15]. Buchner and Baumgartner (2007, Ergonomics 50(7):1036-1063, "Text-background polarity affects performance irrespective of ambient illumination and colour contrast") is the earlier demonstration; only its title/record was verified, not its findings [15]. "Larger for small text" is unverified.
- Newer evidence is more mixed: a 2022 PubMed-indexed review "Dark mode vogue: Do light-on-dark displays have measurable benefits to users?" and a 2023 Ergonomics study (summary only) suggest older adults prefer positive polarity to avoid fatigue while younger adults sometimes prefer negative polarity in dim rooms [15a] (secondary, titles only). Dark mode can still suit users with light sensitivity and dark rooms; the claim that astigmatism (about 30-50% of people, figure unverified) makes light text on dark bloom (halation) was not verified in this pass (unverified). Office rooms are normally bright, so make light the default and offer a dark theme as option, not the default.
- Avoid pure black on pure white (maximal contrast can cause glare for some); use off-white (e.g., #FAFAFA to #F5F5F5 backgrounds) and near-black text (#1A1A1A to #222) while staying above 7:1 (unverified preference; general practice).
- Digital eye strain (computer vision syndrome) is real but its evidence for specific remedies is mixed; the American Optometric Association recommends the 20-20-20 rule (every 20 min look 20 ft away for 20 s) [16] although controlled evidence of benefit is limited (unverified). Reduce glare, avoid large saturated color fields, avoid small low-contrast text.
- Large bright white areas (e.g., full-screen white page images) in a dark theme create strong contrast with chrome; page thumbnails are mostly white, which argues for a light or mid-neutral chrome for the Kontrol tab.

## 10. Information density, progressive disclosure, onboarding and empty states

- **Density**: for expert tools, evidence (e.g., NN/g on dense vs. sparse pages; Tufte's data-ink argument; Shneiderman's visual information seeking mantra "overview first, zoom and filter, details on demand") favours information-rich layouts when the users are trained and the task is repeated [17] (not re-verified; the NN/g and Tufte attributions are unverified; Shneiderman 1996 "The eyes have it" with the mantra "overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand" is cited from memory, unverified). But density must not reduce target size or contrast. Keep row height and font size within accessibility minimums, and gain density through removing redundant chrome, not by shrinking text.
- Offer a density option (Rahat / Normal / Sıkı) with defaults on the comfortable end for this audience.
- **Progressive disclosure**: show common options, reveal advanced ones on demand (Nielsen) [18]. Advanced template options and rarely used filters belong behind a "Gelişmiş" disclosure; limit disclosure to two levels.
- **Onboarding**: for a task-focused internal tool, prefer a short first-run checklist or a guided sample job over a long modal tour; tours have low retention (unverified). Keep the Rehber accessible and contextual.
- **Empty states** (NN/g, Material guidance): each empty state should say what belongs here, why it is empty, and offer the primary action ("Henüz iş yok. Taranmış sayfaları yüklemek için 'Yükle' düğmesine basın."). Never show a blank table.

## 11. Table design for review work

- **Row height**: dense but readable; for older users prefer at least 32-40 px per row with 14 px text (our derivation from the 24 px AA minimum and 44 px AAA targets, not from a study; Material data tables are said to use 52 px default and 36-48 px dense, unverified figures; no NN/g row-height guidance could be fetched).
- **Zebra striping**: evidence is mixed and weak. The A List Apart experiment "Zebra Striping: Does it Really Help?" (2008; author attributed to Jessica Enders, authorship not confirmed this pass) had participants answer three questions on a striped and three on a plain table in randomized order; it found no evidence that striping consistently improves accuracy or speed, although it was the most preferred format (46% preferred stripes, 33% no preference), and the author noted it was one experiment with one dataset. Two follow-up studies found striping improved accuracy on 3 of 8 questions in the second study and was never worse than plain or lined tables [19] (secondary). A critique (Konigi) argued the tested border/background combination handicapped the non-striped tables. A newer controlled study (Ji, Perin, Nacenta, IEEE VIS 2024, "The Effect of Visual Aids on Reading Numeric Data Tables") found colour shading and data bars helped finding maxima, while zebra striping helped more than those for comparing proportional differences [19a] (secondary). The earlier claim that benefit is larger for wide tables is unverified, and no NN/g article on zebra striping or row density could be reached. Use subtle stripes (contrast under 1.2:1 between stripes, not carrying meaning) or thin row dividers; never use stripe color that conflicts with status colors. Keep hover/selection highlight clearly stronger.
- **Number alignment**: right-align numeric columns (or align decimal points); left-align text; headings aligned with their data. Use tabular (monospaced digit) figures for amounts and dates so digits line up. Segoe UI has tabular digits by default in many cases (unverified).
- **Sticky headers**: keep column headers and, for long lists, the first identifying column visible while scrolling; ensure focused row is not covered (2.4.11).
- **Inline editing**: clear affordance (editable cells look like inputs on focus/hover), commit with Enter or Tab, cancel with Esc, validation inline with a message adjacent to the cell, and visible "changed from OCR" marker. Avoid modal edit dialogs for single values. Provide sorting/filtering by status (uncertain, edited, accepted).
- **Row status**: combine icon + text + color; keep the status column in a fixed position.

## 12. Localisation concerns (Turkish)

- **Dotted/dotless I**: Turkish has I/ı and İ/i. Standard ASCII casing ("i".upper() == "I") is wrong. In Python, str.upper()/lower() are locale-independent and give wrong results for Turkish (from Python/Unicode knowledge, not re-fetched); use Qt's QLocale("tr_TR").toUpper()/toLower() or handle I/İ explicitly [20]. Caveat from the Qt 6 docs (verified): QLocale::toUpper uses ICU rules if Qt Core is built with ICU, otherwise a platform-dependent transformation with QString::toUpper as generic fallback, so a Turkish casing test must run on the shipped build, not just be assumed. This matters for sorting, search (case-insensitive matching "ISPARTA" vs "ısparta"), and "ALL CAPS" label styling. Avoid CSS-like uppercase transformations of labels. File name normalisation must be explicit about Turkish characters.
- **Sorting**: use QCollator with the tr_TR locale (ç, ğ, ö, ş, ü sort after c, g, o, s, u, and ı sorts after h and before i, in Turkish alphabet order; from alphabet knowledge, not re-fetched).
- **Text expansion**: Turkish is agglutinative, so words are long, but no Turkish-specific UI expansion figure was found (unverified; measure it on our own strings). General guidance reproduced from IBM's localization table by secondary sources: strings of 1-10 characters expand by 100-200%, falling to about 30% for 71+ characters; Transifex suggests planning for an average of 35%; another source suggests testing with placeholder text 50% longer; one blog claims up to 300% for very short strings without a source [22] (secondary). The earlier "20-30%" and "W3C ... 30-40%" claims are not supported (no W3C string-length guidance was found) and are withdrawn. Design buttons with flexible width and test with the longest strings; avoid fixed-width labels, avoid truncating essential words.
- **Keyboard layouts**: Turkish users have either Turkish-Q or Turkish-F layouts. Wikipedia (via search summary) says the Q layout is the one used on most computers in Turkey despite F's efficiency, and that a 2013 Prime Ministry memorandum required F keyboards in public institutions; Q was still reported popular in 2015 (Daily Sabah) [26] (secondary). No survey with a percentage share was found, so the F share is unverified and may be higher in chambers of commerce, which are public-law bodies (our inference; ask the customer). Shortcut letters are mapped by key position on Q; on F the same letter appears elsewhere. Qt's QKeySequence uses the produced character, so mnemonic letters differ per layout: do not design shortcuts around mnemonic letters only; prefer Ctrl+digits, F-keys, arrows and Enter/Tab/Esc, which are layout-independent. Avoid shortcuts needing characters such as Ç, Ş, Ğ, İ that are awkward on non-Turkish keyboards. Note AltGr combinations (Ctrl+Alt) are produced by AltGr on Turkish keyboards and can collide with character input (e.g., AltGr+Q = @); avoid Ctrl+Alt+letter shortcuts [21] (unverified specifics).
- **Dates, numbers**: Turkish uses dd.MM.yyyy and decimal comma with thousands dot (1.234,56). Use QLocale for formatting and validate typed input in this format.
- **Fonts**: choose fonts that support Turkish glyphs including İ, ı, Ş, ğ with correct diacritics; Segoe UI on Windows does.

## Rules we should adopt for Evrakçı

1. Any action taking over 1 s shows feedback within 100 ms; over 10 s shows a determinate progress bar with "k / n", time remaining and a Cancel button; the UI stays responsive (no GUI-thread blocking above 100 ms).
2. Interactive control hit areas are at least 32x32 px (hard floor 24x24 per WCAG 2.5.8); primary buttons at least 40 px tall; at least 8 px between adjacent targets; destructive buttons separated from primary ones.
3. Body text default is at least 14 px (about 11 pt); table text at least 14 px; no text under 12 px anywhere; in-app UI scale of 100/125/150/200% with no clipped content on 1366x768 at 150%.
4. Text contrast at least 4.5:1 everywhere, 7:1 for body text and table values; UI boundaries/icons/focus/selection at least 3:1 against neighbours.
5. Focus indicator at least 2 px, 3:1 against both the element and the background, visible on every focusable control; the focused row/cell is never hidden by sticky headers or docked panels.
6. Meaning is never conveyed by color alone: every status has an icon plus a Turkish text label.
7. OCR values carry a three-level confidence marker (icon + label) and the table is sortable/filterable by it; "Next uncertain item" has a keyboard shortcut.
8. No silent acceptance: bulk accept shows a count and is undoable; export lists unreviewed/uncertain items and requires an explicit decision.
9. Review includes an audit sample of high-confidence values (configurable, e.g., 5%; value to be tuned) and cross-field validation flags; first N documents per new template are fully reviewed.
10. The page crop for the field being verified sits within the same view as its editable value, with no scrolling or tab switch needed to compare them.
11. Prefer undo over confirmation. Confirmation dialogs only for irreversible actions, with verb-labeled buttons, the safe option as default and the affected count in the message. Undo/redo covers Kontrol, Değerler edits and template edits.
12. Every function has a keyboard path; shortcuts appear in tooltips/menus; a shortcut cheat sheet opens with F1 or Ctrl+/ (test).
13. Shortcuts use layout-independent keys (Ctrl+digit, F-keys, arrows, Enter/Tab/Esc, Ctrl+Z/Y); no shortcut depends on Ç/Ş/Ğ/İ/Ö/Ü, and no Ctrl+Alt+letter shortcuts; single-key shortcuts are inactive while a text field has focus.
14. Modes (draw box, cut mode) display a persistent indicator and a changed cursor, and Esc always returns to neutral.
15. Focus order matches visual order; after accept/commit focus moves to the next unresolved row; Tab and Shift+Tab never trap.
16. Default theme is light with off-white background and near-black text; offer dark and high-contrast themes as options; both themes meet rules 4-5.
17. Tables: right-aligned numerics with tabular digits, sticky header, row height 32-40 px with density option, subtle or no zebra striping (never carrying meaning), inline edit with Enter/Tab/Esc, changed-from-OCR marker.
18. Empty states say what goes here, why it is empty and offer the primary action in Turkish; first run offers a sample job and checklist, not a forced tour.
19. Rare options sit behind one disclosure level ("Gelişmiş"); no more than about 7 primary actions in any toolbar group.
20. Use Turkish-aware casing, sorting and formatting (QLocale tr_TR, QCollator); never use ASCII upper()/lower() on user-visible or matching text; layouts tolerate +50% string length for sentences and up to +100% for labels of 10 characters or fewer (general IBM-style guidance, not Turkish-measured; re-measure on real strings).
21. Show progress for the session ("183 / 612 kontrol edildi") and per-document completion; offer an optional non-modal break reminder (default off).

## Qt/PySide6 feasibility notes

- Threading: QThreadPool + QRunnable or QThread with signals; QProgressBar determinate mode; QProgressDialog has a minimumDuration property (default 4000 ms, verified in Qt 6 docs) so it does not appear for tasks expected to be quicker; set setMinimumDuration deliberately.
- Focus ring: Qt style sheets can style :focus with outline-like borders; QProxyStyle can draw a custom focus rect; test that styled widgets still show a focus indicator, since style sheets often remove it.
- Tables: QTableView with QStyledItemDelegate for inline editors, confidence icons and the changed marker; QHeaderView sticky by default; use setAlternatingRowColors for subtle stripes; setTextAlignment for numerics.
- Sorting: QSortFilterProxyModel with setSortLocaleAware(True) and a tr_TR QCollator-based lessThan.
- Shortcuts: QShortcut/QAction with QKeySequence; use Qt.ShortcutContext.WidgetWithChildrenShortcut and check focus widget for single-key shortcuts. Show them with action text and tooltips.
- Scaling: Qt 6 supports high-DPI by default; for a user text-size setting, scale the application font (QApplication.setFont) and use font-relative sizes (em/pt) in style sheets rather than px.
- Accessibility: Qt exposes UI Automation via QAccessible on Windows; set accessibleName/Description on icon-only buttons.
- Themes: a single QSS template parameterised with colour tokens makes light/dark/high-contrast variants testable; verify contrast programmatically in a test.
- Toasts with undo: a custom overlay widget (QFrame child of the main window) with a QTimer; not natively supported.
